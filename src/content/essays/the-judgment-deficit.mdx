Consider a junior investment analyst hired in 2024. In a previous era, her first two years would have looked something like this: manually building financial models that broke in embarrassing ways, spending late nights reconciling data that didn't reconcile, drafting memos that came back covered in red ink, and sitting through deal meetings where she slowly — painfully — developed an intuition for which numbers mattered and which were noise.

Now consider the same analyst hired in 2026. Her AI tools build the models. They reconcile the data. They draft the memos. She reviews outputs, flags anomalies, and moves faster than her predecessors ever could. She is more productive on day one than the 2024 analyst was after six months.

Here is the question no one is asking: what happens in year ten, when this analyst is the senior person in the room and a decision has to be made that the model can't make? Where does the judgment come from?

The answer is that it may not come from anywhere. Because the messy, frustrating, failure-prone work that built judgment in previous generations has been automated away. Not as a side effect. As the primary value proposition.

This is the judgment deficit. And it is accumulating silently across every organization that has deployed AI without thinking about what it has removed.

## How Judgment Actually Develops

Judgment is not knowledge. Knowledge is knowing that a discounted cash flow model uses a weighted average cost of capital. Judgment is knowing, in a specific context with incomplete information and competing stakeholders, whether the DCF is the right tool at all — and what its outputs actually mean for a decision that has to be made by Thursday.

Judgment develops through a specific mechanism that cognitive science understands well: repeated exposure to ambiguous situations with real consequences, followed by feedback. The key word is ambiguous. Routine tasks build skill. Ambiguous tasks build judgment. The difference matters enormously.

When a junior employee struggles through a messy analysis, makes a wrong call, gets corrected, and tries again, something happens neurologically and experientially that cannot be replicated by reviewing an AI's correct output. The struggle itself is the pedagogy. The failure is the curriculum. Remove the struggle and you have not simply made the work easier — you have removed the mechanism through which expertise develops.

A February 2026 article in Harvard Business Review titled "How Do Workers Develop Good Judgment in the AI Era?" makes this point directly: AI systematically removes the experiences that build judgment in junior workers. The authors argue that the tasks most amenable to AI automation — structured analysis, pattern matching, first-draft synthesis — are the same tasks that historically served as training ground for the unstructured, high-stakes decisions that define senior roles.

This is not a theoretical concern. It is a structural feature of how AI creates value.

## The Deskilling Evidence

The most striking evidence comes from medicine. A 2025 study published by ACM, titled "The AI Deskilling Paradox," examined what happened when AI-assisted diagnostic tools were introduced and then temporarily removed. Endoscopists who had been using AI-assisted detection performed measurably worse when the technology was taken away than they had performed before it was introduced. The AI hadn't just augmented their skill. It had eroded it.

The study revealed an asymmetry that should concern every executive: senior experts with deep existing judgment gained from AI assistance without losing baseline capability. Junior practitioners gained short-term performance but lost the developmental trajectory that would have built independent expertise. AI made the experts better and the novices more dependent.

This maps directly onto organizational life. The senior portfolio manager who uses AI to pressure-test her already-developed thesis gets genuine leverage. The junior analyst who uses AI to generate the thesis he was never forced to develop on his own gets a productivity number that looks great in a quarterly review — and a judgment gap that won't become visible for years.

Meanwhile, the entry-level pipeline is constricting. Fortune and CNBC reported in late 2025 that entry-level job postings plunged 35% between 2023 and 2025. Organizations are discovering that AI can handle much of what entry-level workers used to do. What they have not yet discovered is that entry-level work was never just about the output. It was about building the people who would eventually make the decisions the organization depends on.

## The Oversight Paradox

There is a deeper problem. Organizations that recognize the judgment deficit often respond with what seems like an obvious solution: keep humans in the loop. Let AI do the work, but have humans oversee, review, and validate.

This sounds right. It is structurally flawed.

A Harvard Business School working paper — "The Human-AI Oversight Paradox" — identifies the precise mechanism. The conditions that make AI assistance most valuable are the same conditions that make human oversight least reliable. When cognitive load is high, when decisions are complex, when time pressure is intense — these are exactly the moments when humans gain the most from AI support. They are also the moments when humans are most vulnerable to what the researchers call cognitive substitution: accepting the AI's output without genuine evaluation because the mental resources for independent assessment have been consumed by the complexity of the task itself.

In other words, the harder the problem, the more you need AI help, and the less capable you are of checking whether the AI's help is actually correct.

This is not a training problem. You cannot train people to maintain vigilant oversight under precisely the conditions that make vigilance cognitively impossible. It is a design problem — and most organizations have not begun to design for it.

## The Skeptic's Counter

There is a sophisticated counter-argument, and it deserves serious engagement.

The argument goes like this: AI doesn't eliminate judgment — it transforms it. The modern knowledge worker doesn't need to build judgment through manual analysis any more than a modern pilot needs to build judgment through stick-and-rudder flying in clear weather. The judgment shifts upward. It becomes meta-judgment: the ability to evaluate AI outputs, to know when to trust the model and when to override it, to frame the right questions rather than compute the right answers.

MIT Sloan's October 2025 research, "What's Your Edge?", provides real support for this view. The researchers found that every 1% increase in AI adoption correlated with a 2.5 to 7.5% increase in demand for management roles that emphasize judgment, framing, and what they call "meta-expertise" — the ability to orchestrate human and machine capabilities toward the right problems. AI isn't eliminating the need for judgment. It's concentrating it.

This is partly right. And it is structurally incomplete.

The incompleteness is in the pipeline. Meta-judgment — the ability to evaluate whether an AI's output is subtly wrong, to sense when a model is confidently extrapolating beyond its training distribution, to know which questions the AI isn't being asked — is not a skill that develops in a vacuum. It develops on top of domain judgment. The pilot who can evaluate the autopilot's decisions has thousands of hours of manual flight time underneath that evaluation capability. The doctor who can override the diagnostic AI has years of pattern recognition built through unaided diagnosis.

Meta-expertise is a second-floor capability. You cannot build the second floor without the first.

Gartner's 2026 strategic predictions suggest the market is starting to grasp this: they forecast that by 2028, 50% of organizations will require "AI-free" skills assessments specifically to combat what they term critical thinking atrophy. The need for such assessments is itself an admission that the standard development pipeline is failing.

## The Compounding Problem

Here is what makes the judgment deficit qualitatively different from a typical skills gap: it compounds.

A skills gap is static. The organization lacks a capability, it trains for it, it fills the gap. A judgment deficit is dynamic. Each year that junior employees develop under AI-augmented conditions that remove formative struggle, the pipeline of future senior judgment thins. And unlike a skills gap, the judgment deficit is invisible in current performance metrics. The junior analyst producing AI-augmented work looks, on every measurable dimension, like a high performer. The deficit only reveals itself years later, in a context that doesn't exist yet, on a decision that hasn't been made.

A 2025 meta-analysis published in Nature Human Behaviour provides the empirical foundation for this concern. The researchers found that human-AI collaboration outperforms humans working alone — but on average, it does not outperform AI working alone. The true synergy between humans and AI emerges only on tasks where humans already outperform the AI independently. When humans lack independent capability, adding AI doesn't create a team. It creates a dependency.

Read that finding carefully. It means that the value of human-AI collaboration is a function of independent human capability. Erode the independent capability and you erode the collaboration value. The entire premise of "human in the loop" depends on a reservoir of human judgment that the loop itself is draining.

This creates a compounding dynamic. Year one: AI augments strong human judgment, and the combination is powerful. Year five: the humans whose judgment was built pre-AI begin to retire or promote out. Year ten: the pipeline of independent judgment has thinned significantly. Year fifteen: the organization discovers, in a crisis, that its human-AI system is actually just an AI system with expensive, low-value human reviewers attached.

## The Judgment Pipeline Diagnostic

Most organizations have no visibility into the state of their judgment pipeline. The following five questions are designed to surface the deficit before it becomes a crisis.

**1. What percentage of your junior employees' work involves genuine ambiguity?**

Not AI-assisted ambiguity — actual situations where the employee must make a judgment call without a model to lean on. If the answer is "almost none," the judgment pipeline is already constricting. The goal is not to eliminate AI from junior work. It is to ensure that a meaningful proportion of developmental experiences require independent reasoning under uncertainty.

**2. Can your mid-career professionals perform their core functions without AI tools?**

Not "would they be slower" but "could they do it at all." If removing AI tools would render your five-year employees non-functional rather than merely less efficient, dependency has already displaced development. Run the experiment — even as a tabletop exercise — and observe the results honestly.

**3. Where in your organization do people still learn by failing?**

Identify the last three significant errors made by employees in their first two years. If you can't find any, it's likely not because your junior people are flawless. It's because AI is absorbing the failure that would have been educational. Failure that is caught by AI before it reaches a human is failure that teaches the AI, not the human.

**4. What is the ratio of AI-augmented to AI-independent evaluation in your promotion criteria?**

If every assessment of junior talent is conducted under AI-augmented conditions, the organization is measuring the human-AI system, not the human. It has no signal on independent judgment. This is the organizational equivalent of a student who has never taken a closed-book exam — the transcript looks fine, but no one knows what the student actually knows.

**5. Who owns the judgment pipeline as an organizational asset?**

In most organizations, the answer is "no one." Learning and development owns training. HR owns talent. Business units own performance. But the slow accumulation of judgment across a generation of employees — the pipeline that determines whether the organization will have wise leaders in 2035 — has no owner, no metrics, and no governance. What is unowned is unmanaged. What is unmanaged degrades.

## The Long Horizon

This issue has a particular urgency for organizations that make decisions with multi-decade consequences. A technology company shipping a consumer product can tolerate faster judgment cycles — if something goes wrong, the feedback is quick and the correction can be rapid. But organizations managing pension obligations, infrastructure investments, energy transitions, or sovereign wealth operate on timescales where the consequences of a judgment failure may not materialize for twenty years.

These are precisely the contexts where AI is most useful in the short term — the complexity is enormous, the data volumes are staggering, the analytical burden on humans is genuinely unsustainable. And they are precisely the contexts where the judgment deficit is most dangerous in the long term — because the decisions require a form of wisdom that cannot be algorithmically generated, and the feedback loops are too slow to catch errors before they compound.

The path forward is not to resist AI adoption. That is neither possible nor desirable. The path forward is to recognize that judgment is an organizational asset with a development pipeline, and that AI — for all its extraordinary capabilities — is currently depleting that pipeline as a structural byproduct of the value it creates.

Every organization has an AI strategy. Almost none have a judgment strategy. The former is producing visible, measurable, near-term gains. The absence of the latter is producing an invisible, unmeasurable, long-term deficit.

The gains show up in this quarter's productivity metrics. The deficit shows up in a decision, ten years from now, that someone in the room doesn't have the judgment to make.

By then, it will be too late to build what should have been built all along.
