There's a pattern I've watched repeat across dozens of organizations, including ones run by sophisticated leaders with large budgets and genuine strategic intent. It goes like this:

1. The CEO makes AI a priority
2. A task force forms
3. A vendor is selected
4. A training program launches
5. Adoption looks promising for 90 days
6. Nothing fundamentally changes

The failure isn't in the technology. The failure isn't even in the people. The failure is in the mental model — the assumption that training is the mechanism through which organizations transform.

It isn't. Enablement is.

## The Training Mental Model

Training operates on a simple premise: if people know how to use the tool, they will use it. This premise is wrong in almost every organizational context.

People don't fail to adopt AI because they lack skills. They fail to adopt AI because:
- Their daily routines don't create natural opportunities to use it
- Their managers aren't asking for AI-augmented outputs
- Their performance metrics haven't changed
- The social norms on their team don't yet include AI as a standard practice
- They don't trust that experimental use is safe — career-safe, compliance-safe, reputation-safe

Training addresses exactly one of these barriers: the skill gap. It leaves the other five untouched.

This is why training programs produce a spike-and-decay adoption curve. People learn the tool in a workshop, try it a few times, encounter the friction of their unchanged work environment, and revert to established patterns. The tool becomes available but optional — and in organizations, optional means unused.

## The Enablement Mental Model

Enablement starts from a fundamentally different premise: **transformation is an environmental design problem, not an information delivery problem.**

The question isn't "Do people know how to use AI?" The question is "Does the organizational environment actively support and reinforce new ways of working?"

This reframe changes everything about program design.

### Environment Design Means Multiple Interventions

At CPP Investments, we designed four interlocking pillars — not because four is a nice number, but because organizational behavior has multiple barriers that require distinct interventions:

**Hackathons** address the *awareness and permission* barrier. When 2,200 people participate in a company-wide hackathon and see their colleagues building AI solutions, the psychological barrier to experimentation drops dramatically. It becomes normal. And normal is more powerful than any training module.

**Sprints** address the *depth and capability* barrier. A week-long immersive — with kickstart labs, protected focus time, daily 1:1 coaching, and real business problems — does what no workshop can: it compresses months of self-directed learning into a focused experience where people build genuine skill under expert guidance.

**The Leadership Council** addresses the *alignment and reinforcement* barrier. If middle management isn't actively supporting new behaviors, those behaviors won't survive first contact with operational pressure. The council — reporting directly to the COO — ensures that enablement isn't a side project; it's connected to how the firm operates.

**Governance pathways** address the *confidence and scale* barrier. People won't invest serious effort in AI solutions if they believe those solutions will die in a sandbox. Governed pathways from prototype to production — connecting AI products, vendor partnerships, and Responsible AI guardrails — give people confidence that what they build can actually matter.

## The Diagnostic Question

If you lead an AI program, here's the diagnostic question: **what percentage of your investment goes to information delivery versus environment design?**

If the answer is predominantly information delivery — webinars, workshops, e-learning modules, how-to guides — you're training. And training alone will not transform your organization.

Environment design means investing in:
- **Experiential programs** that create new norms (hackathons, sprints, communities of practice)
- **Leadership structures** that reinforce new behaviors (enablement councils, executive coaching, management KPIs)
- **Governance systems** that build trust (Responsible AI frameworks, scaling pathways, risk-aware product management)
- **Measurement systems** that track behavioral change, not just tool usage

## The Compounding Effect

The most important property of an enablement architecture is that its components compound. A hackathon participant who then joins a sprint who then gets support from a governance pathway who then sees their manager reinforcing AI-augmented work on the council's direction — that person doesn't just adopt AI. They become an agent of organizational change themselves.

Training produces tool users. Enablement produces behavioral change at scale. The distinction isn't semantic. It's the difference between organizations that talk about AI transformation and organizations that actually achieve it.
