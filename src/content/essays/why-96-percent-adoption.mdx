When we hit 96% adoption across roughly 2,000 employees at CPP Investments, I got a lot of congratulations. Board members nodded approvingly. Peers at other organizations asked how we did it.

And my honest reaction was: this is the least interesting number in the story.

## The Adoption Illusion

Adoption — measured as the percentage of employees who have used an AI tool at least once — tells you almost nothing about organizational transformation. It tells you that people logged in. It tells you that the friction to first use was low enough. It tells you that your change management wasn't actively hostile.

What it doesn't tell you is whether anyone is working differently.

I've seen organizations with 80%+ adoption where the dominant use case is "help me write an email." That's not transformation. That's a slightly faster typewriter. The underlying systems of work — how decisions get made, how analysis gets structured, how teams collaborate — remain untouched.

## What Actually Matters: Tier Migration

The metric I care about is **tier migration** — the measurable shift in how deeply and frequently people engage with AI tools over time.

We track four tiers:
- **Explorers**: Occasional use, basic prompts, surface-level engagement
- **Regular users**: Weekly engagement, building prompts into existing workflows
- **Daily users**: AI is integrated into daily decision-making and work production
- **Power users**: Redesigning workflows around AI capabilities, building custom solutions, mentoring others

The number that matters isn't the total adoption figure. It's the velocity at which people move from Explorer to Daily User — and whether that movement sustains or decays.

At CPP Investments, we've seen the mix shift measurably toward daily and super users, with approximately 45% at peak operating at daily-use levels or above. That's not an awareness metric. That's a behavioral indicator that systems of work are actually changing.

## Why Most Enablement Programs Plateau

Most enterprise AI programs follow a predictable arc: executive enthusiasm, a pilot program, a training initiative, an adoption dashboard that looks good for a quarter, and then a plateau. Usage stabilizes at "good enough," leadership attention moves to the next priority, and the organization settles into a pattern where AI is available but not transformative.

The reason is structural. Training teaches people *how* to use a tool. It doesn't redesign the *context* in which they work. If someone's daily routine, incentive structure, team norms, and management expectations remain unchanged, a training program — no matter how well designed — is just information delivery.

Enablement is different. Enablement changes the environment:

- **Hackathons** create social proof and lower the psychological barrier to experimentation
- **Sprints** compress months of individual learning into focused weeks with coaching and peer accountability
- **Leadership alignment** ensures that managers are reinforcing new behaviors, not passively allowing them
- **Governance pathways** give people confidence that the solutions they're building will actually be supported and scaled

Each of these interventions addresses a different barrier. Together, they create the reinforcing loops that sustain behavioral change beyond the initial excitement.

## The Question for Every Executive

If you're tracking AI adoption in your organization, ask yourself this: are you measuring awareness, or are you measuring behavioral shift?

If your dashboard shows high adoption but you can't point to specific workflows, decisions, or team structures that have fundamentally changed as a result — you're celebrating the wrong metric.

The organizations that will actually capture value from AI are the ones that move past adoption as a vanity metric and start measuring the thing that actually matters: are people building new systems of work, or just using new tools to do old work faster?

The answer requires honesty. And it requires an enablement architecture designed for behavioral change — not just tool distribution.
